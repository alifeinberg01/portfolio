Does Homework Actually Help?

Homework is one of those things schools do because schools have always done it. It shows up in syllabi, grade-books, and parent expectations as a given - rarely as a decision. But when I actually asked myself why we assign it and whether it works, I found the honest answer is far messier than the tradition suggests, and that mess has real consequences for equity. My argument is this: homework helps, but only sometimes, only in the right amount, and deeply unequally. Because the benefit depends so heavily on grade level, quality, and what a student has waiting for them at home, homework is not a neutral default. It is a policy choice, and administrators should make it on purpose, around quality and equity, rather than inherit it unexamined.

What the research actually shows

The most careful synthesis on this question comes from Cooper et al. (2006), who reviewed studies from 1987 to 2003 and found a clear pattern: the link between homework and achievement is positive for students in grades 7-12 and weak to negligible for elementary students. In other words, the younger the child, the less homework does, and the more it depends on an adult at home to make it work. Kohn (2006) goes further, arguing that much of what we assign is busywork that teaches compliance rather than learning and erodes children’s love of school. I do not fully agree with Kohn’s wholesale rejection, but he forces the right question: if a task does not clearly help, what is it actually for?

Does homework improve learning, or just measure who has support at home?

This is the question that unsettles me most as a future administrator. When a student completes a strong homework assignment, how much of that success belongs to the assignment and how much to the quiet house, the parent who can explain fractions, the reliable internet, and the desk to work at? For many of my students and families, none of those supports are guaranteed. When we grade homework, we risk grading a child’s home circumstances and calling it their learning. That is not an argument against all homework; it is an argument for being honest about what a homework grade represents, and for separating the practice of learning from the penalty for a disadvantaged setting. A student who cannot do homework at home has not learned less they have been handed a harder version of the same task.

If we assign less, what should take its place?

It is too easy to stop at “assign less,” because that can simply mean less practice for students who genuinely need it. The harder leadership question is what fills the gap. The research points toward a few answers: protect in-school time for guided practice so students are not sent home to struggle alone; make whatever homework we do assign short, purposeful, and about practice rather than new learning; and build in school-based supports - study halls, homework clubs, advisory time - so a student’s access to help does not depend on their zip code. Bassot (2016) describes reflective practice as interrupting our automatic routines long enough to ask whether they still serve their purpose. Homework is a textbook automatic routine. A reflective leader does not abolish it reflexively; they redesign it intentionally.

Why this is an instructional-leadership and equity (CAPE 2 and CAPE 4) issue

This theme belongs to CAPE Standard 2: Instructional Leadership, because a schoolwide homework policy is fundamentally a curriculum-and-assessment decision about how students practice and how we measure learning. It is equally a CAPE Standard 4: Family and Community Engagement issue, because homework is the single most consistent point of contact between school expectations and the home, and the place where inequities in home resources show up most plainly. A leader who sets homework policy without thinking about the range of homes those assignments land in is not being neutral; they are quietly advancing over the already-advantaged.

Key Takeaways

  • Homework helps only sometimes. The benefit depends on grade level, quality, and amount.
  • A homework grade can measure a student’s home circumstances as much as their learning.
  • Assigning less is not enough. Protect in-school practice time and build school-based supports to fill the gap.
  • Homework is a policy choice, and leaders should make it on purpose, around quality and fairness.
