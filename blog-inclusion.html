What Does Inclusion Really Mean?

As an Education Specialist, I have watched the word “inclusion” get used to describe two completely different things. Sometimes it means a student with a disability is genuinely part of the life of a classroom - learning alongside peers, supported, belonging. And sometimes it means a student is simply placed in a general-education room, sitting at the back with an aide, working on something unrelated, present but not included. We call both “inclusion,” and that slippage is exactly the problem. My argument, from inside this work, is that inclusion is not a place or a placement, it is a set of adult decisions and practices. Putting a student with a disability in a general-education class is proximity, not inclusion. Real inclusion is built by leaders through scheduling, staffing, co-teaching, and a culture of belonging; it is a schoolwide leadership responsibility, not a favor granted by a placement decision or a burden handed to the special-education teacher alone.

The evidence is stronger than most people assume

One reason inclusion gets treated as a nice-to-have is a lingering belief that it helps students with disabilities socially but costs them, or their peers, academically. The research does not support that. Hehir et al. (2016), in a large international summary of the evidence, found that students with disabilities educated in inclusive settings generally do better academically and socially than those in segregated settings - and, importantly, that including them does not harm the achievement of their peers without disabilities. Theoharis (2007), studying principals who pursued equity, found that genuine inclusion did not happen by accident or goodwill; it required leaders who deliberately restructured their schools and who persisted against real resistance to do it. And the legal foundation has said as much for decades: the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (2004) requires education in the least restrictive environment, with removal from general education only when the nature or severity of the disability is such that learning there cannot be achieved satisfactorily even with supplementary aids and services. The law, in other words, already assumes inclusion is the default and segregation the exception that must be justified - a standard many schools quietly invert.

Is a student who is physically present but unsupported actually included?

I would argue no, and this is the heart of the matter. A student sitting in a classroom they cannot access is not included - they are isolated in a crowd, which can be lonelier than a separate room. Inclusion is not measured by where a desk sits; it is measured by whether the student is learning, participating, and belonging. That distinction has enormous implications for leaders, because it means inclusion cannot be achieved by a placement decision on an IEP form. It is achieved only by the daily work that follows: accessible materials, trained staff, co-planning time, and teachers who see that student as theirs. When we count placement as inclusion, we let ourselves off the hook for the much harder work that real inclusion demands.

Whose job is inclusion: The special-education teacher’s, or the whole school’s?

In too many buildings, inclusion is treated as the special-education department’s private project something that happens to general education rather than with it. I have lived on the receiving end of that arrangement, and it does not work. When inclusion belongs to one teacher or one department, it collapses the moment that person is stretched too thin. Theoharis’s (2007) principals succeeded precisely because they made inclusion a schoolwide commitment, shared ownership, shared schedules, shared responsibility for every student. As a future administrator, this is the shift I most want to lead: moving inclusion from “the SPED teacher’s job” to “how our school works.” Bassot (2016) describes reflective practice as examining the assumptions we have stopped noticing, and “inclusion is the special-ed teacher’s job” is exactly that kind of invisible assumption - one a reflective leader has to name and dismantle.

Key Takeaways

  • Inclusion is not a place or a placement. It is a set of adult decisions and practices.
  • The research shows inclusive settings benefit students with disabilities without harming their peers.
  • A student who is physically present but unsupported is in proximity, not included.
  • Inclusion is a schoolwide leadership responsibility, not the special-education teacher’s job alone.
