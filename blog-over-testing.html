Are We Over-Testing Our Students?

If you add it all up - state assessments, interim benchmarks, district screeners, curriculum-embedded unit tests, English-language proficiency testing, and the practice tests we give to get ready for the real tests, a lot of the school year is spent measuring students instead of teaching them. I feel this acutely in special education, where my students are often pulled for still more assessment on top of everything their general-education peers take. Somewhere along the way, testing stopped being a tool and became the schedule. My argument is not that we test too much and should test less, full stop. It is more specific: schools have confused the amount of testing with the usefulness of testing, and the administrator’s real job is not to add tests or defend them, but to curate them, cutting the low-value, high-stakes testing that narrows what teachers can teach, while fiercely protecting the small, daily, classroom-level assessment that actually moves learning. Not all testing is equal, and treating it as if it were is itself a leadership failure.

The research points in a surprising direction

Here is the part that reframed the issue for me. The assessment with the strongest evidence behind it is also the cheapest and least disruptive. Black and Wiliam’s (1998) landmark review found that formative assessment - the quick, in-the-moment checks a teacher uses to adjust a lesson - produces some of the largest learning gains in all of education, especially for lower-achieving students. Meanwhile, Nichols and Berliner (2007) documented how high-stakes standardized testing, when it becomes the dominant measure, actively corrupts instruction - narrowing the curriculum, squeezing out untested subjects, and pushing teachers toward test prep rather than teaching. So the evidence does not say “assessment is bad.” It says we have been pouring time and anxiety into the least useful kind while under-investing in the most useful kind.

Is all this testing telling us something we can actually use to help a child?

This is the question I would want to ask about every assessment on our calendar. A test earns its place only if a specific adult will do something specific with the result for a specific student. Much of our high-stakes testing fails that test: the scores arrive months later, after the student has moved on, and they tell a teacher what they already knew from working with the child every day. As a future administrator, I would use that question as a filter: for each assessment, who uses the data, how soon, and to change what? If there is no answer, the test is costing us instructional time without buying us anything.

What are students and teachers not doing during the hours we spend testing?

Every hour has an opportunity cost, and in schools that cost is paid in teaching. When a week is consumed by benchmark testing, that is a week of instruction, intervention, projects, and relationship-building that did not happen. Bassot (2016), in our course text, describes reflective practice as deliberately stepping back to examine the routines we have stopped questioning. Testing calendars are exactly that kind of unexamined routine - they accrete year after year, each test added for a good reason, until no one has asked whether the whole pile is still worth it. A reflective leader has to be willing to open that calendar and ask what it is crowding out. For my students with disabilities, the answer is often the specialized instruction and practice they need most, lost to assessments that were never designed with them in mind.

Why this is an instructional-leadership (CAPE 2) issue

This theme lives in CAPE Standard 2: Instructional Leadership, specifically Element 2B, Promoting Effective Curriculum, Instruction, and Assessment, which asks leaders to ensure assessment actually supports teaching and learning for every student. It also connects to CAPE Standard 1: Development and Implementation of a Shared Vision, because deciding what we measure is really a statement about what we value. If our calendar says we value compliance scores over daily learning, our vision is hollow. The leadership move is not louder complaints about testing; it is the quieter, harder work of auditing what we give, protecting formative assessment, and making sure the data we collect comes back to classrooms in time to help a child.

Key Takeaways

  • Not all testing is equal. The amount of testing a school does is not the same as how useful that testing is.
  • Quick, classroom-level formative assessment has the strongest evidence behind it, and it is the kind most worth protecting.
  • Every assessment should earn its place on the calendar: who uses the data, how soon, and to change what?
  • Leaders cannot eliminate state testing, but they can audit what sits on top of it and give that time back to teaching.
